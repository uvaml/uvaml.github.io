**Abstract**
While peptides have been used clinically for decades, advances in synthetic chemistry have enabled a new generation of synthetically complex peptides that is rapidly transforming modern medicine and driving a therapeutic industry exceeding $40 billion. Unlike early peptide drugs composed solely of linear sequences of the 20 canonical amino acids, most recently approved peptides incorporate non-natural chemical modifications such as backbone cyclization, non-natural side chains, polymer or peptoid units, and D-amino acids. These modifications improve stability, bioavailability, and target specificity. At the same time, they dramatically expand the chemical design space, making rational optimization increasingly difficult. Machine learning offers a promising route to navigate this complexity, but current approaches remain limited. Most models represent peptides as simple sequences of the 20 natural amino acids. As a result, they cannot accommodate non-standard residues or non-linear architectures such as macrocycles and stapled peptides. This mismatch restricts their usefulness for modern synthetic peptide design.

To address these challenges, we developed two complementary software tools that enable machine learning on synthetically complex peptides. First, we introduce PepMNet, a multi-scale graph neural network that represents peptides at both the atomic and residue levels. This dual representation captures fine-grained chemical features while preserving higher-level structural information, allowing the model to natively encode non-natural residues and complex topologies. Across multiple benchmarking tasks, including antimicrobial activity prediction and chromatographic retention time estimation, PepMNet outperforms existing sequence-based methods, particularly in settings where non-natural residues are uncommon or poorly sampled. Second, we developed PepFoundry, an open-source toolkit that streamlines the curation and preprocessing of synthetically modified peptide datasets. PepFoundry converts non-standard peptide sequences into machine learning-ready graph representations, enabling consistent data handling and accelerating model development.

As a proof of concept, we have applied PepMNet and PepFoundry to the discovery of antimicrobial peptides in collaboration with the Hughes Lab at the University of Virginia Medical School. We curated a dataset of synthetically complex peptides containing non-natural side chains, polymer units, and D-amino acids with measured activity against Klebsiella pneumoniae, a WHO priority pathogen [3]. By pretraining on large public datasets and fine-tuning on this clinically relevant set, our model identified highly potent candidates. Coupling PepMNet with a genetic algorithm enabled efficient exploration of chemical space, yielding novel sequences predicted to exhibit strong antimicrobial activity. Top candidates were synthesized and experimentally evaluated using minimum inhibitory concentration assays.

Together, these tools provide an integrated computational framework for representing, predicting, and optimizing synthetically complex peptides, enabling data-driven discovery of next-generation therapeutics across increasingly diverse chemical landscapes.


**Bio:**
Dr. Bilodeau received her B.S. and M.S. from Northwestern University and her Ph.D. from Rensselaer Polytechnic Institute, both in Chemical and Biological Engineering. During her Ph.D., she received the Lawrence Livermore Advanced Simulations and Computation Graduate Fellowship, through which she carried out research at Lawrence Livermore National Laboratory. Dr. Bilodeau was a postdoctoral researcher studying machine learning for molecular design in Klavs Jensen’s group in the Chemical Engineering department at MIT. She joined the University of Virginia as an assistant professor in Chemical Engineering and began her position in January 2023.

Molecular simulations are powerful tools that can predict important physical properties while simultaneously yielding a full molecular picture of the system. In the past decade, the advent of GPU computing has resulted in dramatic improvements in the computational speed of molecular simulations, making it possible to study larger, more complex systems. At the same time, the field of deep learning has experienced a renaissance, with neural networks being successfully employed for reading text, classifying images, and even folding proteins. This convergence of increased hardware and algorithmic capabilities has set the stage for combining the wealth of data arising from rapid molecular simulations with the deep learning tools to mine this data.

In the Bilodeau group, we explore the intersection between molecular simulations, statistical physics, and artificial intelligence to develop tools to discover and design new molecules, surfaces, and proteins with optimized properties. Our core expertise lies in molecular dynamics simulations of soft matter systems and artificial intelligence for molecular property prediction and generation. This interdisciplinary toolset allows us to solve important problems in applications ranging from designing biotherapeutics to developing novel separation materials.
